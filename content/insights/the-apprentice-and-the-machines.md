On 27 May 1471, Florence watched a gilded copper ball rise to the very top of its cathedral.

The dome underneath it was already famous. Filippo Brunelleschi had finished it in 1436, after sixteen years of work that most people had said could not be done. The lantern on top followed after his death. The ball was the last piece, and the job of making it and putting it up went to the workshop of Andrea del Verrocchio.

Verrocchio's crew had a problem Brunelleschi had already solved. To get tons of brick and stone up to the dome, he had invented his own machines: a hoist driven by oxen that could lift at three different speeds and switch from raising to lowering without the oxen ever turning round, and a revolving crane to set heavy pieces precisely in place. Those machines were still on site. So a workshop in 1471 lifted its ball with equipment designed by a man who had died in 1446.

One of the people on that job was a nineteen-year-old apprentice called Leonardo.

## What Leonardo took from it

Leonardo spent about four years in Verrocchio's workshop, and he used the job to study Brunelleschi's machines closely. His drawings of the crane used to place the ball, and of the three-speed hoist, still survive: two sheets in the Codex Atlanticus and another at the Biblioteca Ambrosiana in Milan.

A decade later, in 1482, he wrote to the ruler of Milan asking for a job. The letter is one of the earliest CVs we have, and most of it is engineering: portable bridges, cannons, tunnels, armoured wagons. Painting comes near the end, almost as an afterthought: "Also, in painting, I can do as much as anyone, whoever he may be."

The greatest painter of his age sold himself as an engineer. And a good part of that engineering he had learned by using another man's machines, on a real job, next to people who knew how to run them.

## Three things Florence got right

I keep coming back to this story because it describes, better than most business books, how a new technology actually takes hold in an organisation.

**The tools outlived their inventor.** Brunelleschi's machines were still working a generation after he died, because the cathedral works kept them, maintained them and kept using them. Most companies lose their best tools the moment the person who built them leaves: the spreadsheet only one person understands, the automation nobody else can fix, the prompt library that lived on someone's laptop.

**The know-how lived in the workshop.** Verrocchio's crew did not need Brunelleschi in the room. The knowledge of how to use his machines had been passed on in the work itself, from the people who ran them to the people who would run them next. That is the part most AI programmes skip. They buy the tool, run a training day, and leave the knowledge in a few people's heads.

**People learned on real work.** Leonardo did not learn engineering in a classroom. He learned it on a job the whole city was watching, alongside people who had done it before. Every serious adoption I have seen works the same way: one real workflow, done properly, with the people who do the job, and the skills spread from there.

## What this means for AI

We are, in a small way, in Brunelleschi's position again. The machines are new and powerful, and most organisations do not yet know how to lift anything with them.

The temptation is to treat that as a purchasing decision, and buying is the easy part. The harder questions are where the knowledge of how to use them will live, who will keep them running when the person who set them up moves on, and who is learning from whom on real work.

That is the thinking behind how we work at Quartiere: start with one real workflow, build with the people who do the job, and make sure what they learn stays in the building. It is also why we built a company brain in the first place. The know-how of a business should not disappear when someone goes on holiday, let alone when they retire.

Florence understood this five hundred years ago. The machines mattered. The workshop that kept them running mattered more.

> Lightning brought the ball down in 1601. Florence had a new one on top of the dome by October 1602. A city that keeps its know-how can rebuild what it loses.
