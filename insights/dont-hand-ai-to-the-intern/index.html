Don't hand AI to the intern

Roald van Buuren · 23 September 2026

Around 2010, I watched a lot of companies discover social media.

The pattern was almost always the same. Leadership knew it mattered. They had read the articles, seen the numbers, heard that their competitors were "doing something" with it. What they didn't know was what to actually do.

So they found the youngest person in the building with a Twitter account and made it their job.

I spent those years in gaming, where social media wasn't a side channel. It was where the audience lived, where the product got judged, and where a bad decision could turn into a crisis before lunch. From that side of the fence, it was strange to watch other industries treat it as a task for whoever had the most free time.

The companies that did that got a Twitter account. Some of them got a funny one.

The companies that pulled ahead did something else. They let it change how they worked. Customer service started answering there. Product teams started listening there. Marketing stopped broadcasting and started talking. Hiring, sales and even crisis planning shifted around it. None of that happens when it belongs to one junior person with no authority over anything else.

That was the real difference. One group added a channel. The other group changed the operating model.

The same moment, again

I see exactly the same pattern with AI right now, and I see it in almost every company I talk to.

Leadership knows it matters. There is budget. There are licences. There is usually a pilot, often run by the most enthusiastic person on the team, sometimes literally the intern. There is a chatbot that answers questions about the holiday policy.

And the work itself hasn't changed.

Meanwhile, half the company is using AI on their own phones, for their own tasks, completely outside anything the business can see or build on. The people who aren't using it are quietly worried it's coming for their job, which is a very reasonable thing to worry about when nobody has told you otherwise.

That combination (a pilot nobody depends on, private use nobody can learn from, and a workforce that is mostly anxious) is what "handing it to the intern" looks like in 2026.

Why it keeps happening

It isn't laziness. It's that AI looks like a tool, so companies treat it like a tool purchase. Buy the licence, run the training, tick the box.

But the value of AI doesn't sit in the tool. It sits in the workflow: who asks what, where the information lives, who makes which decision, what gets checked by a human and what doesn't. Change none of that, and the best model in the world becomes one more tab nobody opens.

Gaming taught me this the hard way. Players adopt whatever makes them better at the thing they care about, and they quietly route around everything else. Employees are exactly the same. If the new system makes their day easier, they will use it without being asked. If it adds a step, they will find a way around it, no matter how many all-hands presentations you give.

Adoption is the whole game.

What to do instead

A few things I'd push any leadership team to do before buying another licence:

Start from the work, not the tool. Pick one real workflow that matters (quoting, support, onboarding, a recurring decision) and map how it actually happens today, including the bits nobody writes down.

Give it an owner with authority. Someone who can change the process, not just the software. That is rarely the most junior person in the room.

Design with the people who do the job. They know where the friction is. They also need to see that the goal is more capacity from the team you already have, not fewer people.

Make one thing work properly before you make ten things half work. One system people genuinely rely on is worth more than a dozen pilots.

Measure use, not launches. A tool nobody uses has delivered nothing, however good the demo was.

The same lesson, fifteen years later

The social media wave eventually sorted itself out. The companies that treated it as a side project mostly caught up, years later, at a higher cost, usually after a competitor had already taken their audience.

AI will sort itself out too. The difference is the stakes. This time it isn't a marketing channel. It is how the work itself gets done.

So by all means, involve the intern. They'll probably have great ideas.

Just don't make it their problem alone.
