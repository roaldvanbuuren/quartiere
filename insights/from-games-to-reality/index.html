Insights / Research

From Games to Reality

How gaming is becoming a system for measuring, training and modelling human behaviour.

Roald van Buuren · 25 August 2026
Executive summary

Gaming is becoming a behavioural laboratory.

For most of its history, the relationship between games and the real world has been framed around transfer: do the skills developed in games make somebody better at something outside the game? That question is useful, but it is becoming too narrow.

Games are increasingly being used to train people, select people, measure people, change behaviour, solve problems and study how people behave under conditions that are difficult to reproduce in the physical world.

A sufficiently complex game creates an environment in which humans repeatedly make decisions while operating under rules, incentives, uncertainty, incomplete information, time pressure, resource constraints and the actions of other people. Unlike most conventional assessments, games can record the sequence of those decisions rather than simply the final outcome.

A CV records where someone has been. An interview records what someone says they would do. A conventional test samples behaviour under a small number of artificial conditions. A game can record thousands of decisions made while a person is solving problems, adapting to failure, allocating attention, managing risk and responding to changing circumstances.

Games can create unusually rich behavioural environments in which human decision-making becomes observable, repeatable and measurable.
ApplicationCore question
TrainingCan a game change performance in another environment?
SelectionCan gameplay identify people likely to perform well in a real-world role?
MeasurementCan gameplay reveal behavioural characteristics that conventional assessment misses?
InterventionCan changing rules, incentives or consequences change behaviour?
ModellingCan gameplay data help predict how people will behave in unfamiliar situations?
Train people → observe people → measure people → model people → simulate people.

The final step remains largely experimental. If behavioural models can be derived from sufficiently rich gameplay, games become potential laboratories for building models of human decision-making that can subsequently be placed inside simulations of real-world systems.

01 / Transfer

The problem with the traditional transfer argument

The popular version of the gaming thesis is simple: games teach skills, therefore gamers have useful skills.

The evidence is more complicated. Gaming does not produce one general-purpose cognitive advantage. Different games repeatedly exercise different behavioural systems.

A competitive FPS may require rapid visual acquisition, target tracking and spatial prediction. A strategy game may require resource allocation and long-horizon planning. A cooperative game may require coordination and communication. An MMO may require social organisation. A flight simulator may require attention management and procedural learning.

The useful unit of analysis is therefore not gamer but behaviour performed under a particular set of environmental constraints.

02 / Behavioural environments

Why games are unusually useful behavioural environments

Traditional experiments often isolate variables by removing complexity. Real life does the opposite. A person making a decision at work, in an aircraft, in a hospital or on a battlefield is dealing with multiple interacting variables at once.

Games can occupy the middle ground. They can preserve complexity while keeping the environment observable and repeatable.

A game can specify objectives, rules, rewards, penalties, information availability, resource constraints, time limits, social relationships, environmental conditions, failure states and feedback loops. And, critically, it can record what happens.

State → decision → action → consequence → adaptation → next decision.
03–14 / Case studies

Twelve cases where the boundary starts to disappear.

03 / FAA

The FAA goes looking for gamers

In 2026, the Federal Aviation Administration explicitly launched a recruitment campaign aimed at gamers as part of its effort to recruit the next generation of air traffic controllers. The FAA identified characteristics associated with gaming as relevant to air traffic control, including cognitive function, multitasking, spatial awareness, strategy and problem-solving. The deeper question is whether gameplay can identify people whose existing behavioural repertoire overlaps with the demands of the role.

04 / DRONE OPERATORS

Gamers as drone operators

Research comparing experienced pilots, experienced video game players and controls on tasks relevant to unmanned aerial systems found that pilots outperformed gamers on some complex multi-attribute cognitive tasks, while gamers performed strongly on visual acquisition, identification and target tracking. Gamers and pilots performed similarly on simulated UAS landing tasks. The interface between human and machine is changing, and gaming has spent decades training people to operate through screen-based, sensor-mediated interfaces.

05 / SPACE FORTRESS

Space Fortress and abstract skill transfer

Space Fortress was developed as a controlled environment for studying complex skill acquisition. Research associated with military aviation found evidence that structured training using the game could improve subsequent aviation performance. The game did not look like an aircraft. The relevant skills were abstract: attention management, competing priorities, situational awareness and workload control.

06 / SURGERY

Surgery and the value of controlled failure

A randomized controlled trial tested whether game-based training could improve surgical residents' ability to recognise and solve equipment problems. The game-trained group solved a median of 59% of standardized malfunctions compared with 33% for controls. The game was training situational awareness under disruption, creating repeated exposure to failure states that are expensive and difficult to reproduce physically.

07 / EMERGENCY MEDICINE

Emergency medicine and changing heuristics

A randomized controlled trial compared a serious game with traditional educational material for trauma triage. Physicians who played the game under-triaged severely injured patients 53% of the time compared with 64% among the traditional education group. Six months later the difference remained: 57% versus 74%. The mechanism looks more like decision, consequence, recognition of error, revised mental model.

08 / WORLD OF WARCRAFT

World of Warcraft and the accidental pandemic

The 2005 Corrupted Blood incident became an accidental behavioural laboratory. A disease mechanic escaped its intended raid environment and spread through the wider game world. Players fled, helped infected players, attempted quarantine, deliberately spread the infection and experimented with the system. The event was not a miniature human pandemic, but it demonstrated that human behaviour can generate emergent system-level effects inside a virtual environment.

09 / SEA HERO QUEST

Sea Hero Quest and measuring people at scale

Sea Hero Quest was deliberately built as a scientific measurement instrument. More than 4.3 million people played it, generating tens of millions of sessions and a population-scale dataset of navigation behaviour. Research found ecological validity between game performance and real-world wayfinding behaviour. The game became a population-scale behavioural test.

10 / FOLDIT

Foldit and human problem-solving

Foldit reversed the relationship between game and reality. Researchers turned a scientific problem into a game, and players used the environment to solve protein-folding problems. Players produced a structure that helped researchers solve the Mason-Pfizer monkey virus protease problem. The lesson is that a game can turn human intuition, pattern recognition and spatial reasoning into computationally useful search.

11 / EVE ONLINE

EVE Online and distributed intelligence

Project Discovery embeds scientific classification inside a persistent commercial game. One Human Protein Atlas implementation generated nearly 33 million classifications from more than 322,000 players in its first year. The game becomes an interface between human cognition and scientific infrastructure.

12 / MMO LEADERSHIP

MMO behaviour and offline leadership

A study involving more than 18,000 MMO players examined the relationship between in-game behaviour and offline leadership. Relationship-oriented behaviour was associated with leadership in voluntary organisations, while task-oriented behaviour had weaker relationships and did not generalise to company leadership. The result demonstrates both signal and boundary.

13 / HUMAN-AI

Games as laboratories for human-AI collaboration

The Overcooked environment has become a useful testbed for human-AI coordination. Agents trained through self-play can perform extremely well with other AI agents while performing worse when paired with humans. Agents that explicitly model human behaviour can coordinate more effectively. Games provide a controlled laboratory for human compatibility with autonomous systems.

14 / DECISION MODELLING

From observing decisions to predicting them

Researchers have demonstrated that sequential human decisions can be modelled from behavioural datasets. One study assembled more than 168,000 decisions from Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma experiments and used recurrent neural networks to model behavioural trajectories. More recent work studies how people reason when encountering entirely unfamiliar games. The harder question is whether behavioural regularities can predict decision classes in new environments.

15 / Military

The military is already moving in this direction.

Military use provides an unusually clear demonstration because the cost of real-world experimentation is high.

The US Army has used immersive simulations to train command, communication and decision-making. Army systems have also used virtual humans and game-based scenarios to train junior leaders in interpersonal communication and counselling.

DARPA's Strategic Social Interaction Modules programme targeted the human dynamics required for military personnel operating as negotiators, peacekeepers, law-enforcement personnel and relief workers. Its objectives included entering unfamiliar social environments, recovering from social errors, de-escalating conflict and adapting to unfamiliar behavioural rules.

18 / Behaviour is the data

The central insight: behaviour is the data.

The traditional game industry thinks primarily in terms of players, engagement, retention and monetisation. The research reviewed here suggests another interpretation.

A game generates players → decisions → interactions → adaptations → behavioural trajectories.

That is a different asset. The valuable object is not necessarily the game. It is the behavioural dataset produced by the game.

Traditional assessment generally observes fragments. Games can observe trajectories.
19 / Context

The critical caveat: behaviour is contextual.

A player's behaviour is shaped by the environment. A player may cooperate because cooperation is rewarded, behave aggressively because aggression is rewarded, take risks because failure has little cost, or optimise the game rather than the intended objective.

The relevant question is not what gameplay tells us about who someone is. It is what behavioural tendencies a person exhibits under particular conditions, and which of those tendencies remain stable when the conditions change.

Transfer therefore has to be demonstrated, not assumed.

20 / The CV

What should replace the CV?

The emerging opportunity is not necessarily to replace the CV with gaming. It is to move from static credentials toward observed behaviour.

The hiring stack could eventually look more like: CV → psychometrics → work sample → behavioural simulation → longitudinal performance model.

A work sample asks someone to perform a task. A behavioural simulation can place someone inside a changing environment and observe how they respond. One samples performance. The other generates a trajectory.

21 / Decision intelligence

The emerging opportunity for decision intelligence.

Imagine a simulation of a future battlefield with 10,000 synthetic actors. Each has different risk tolerance, trust thresholds, information-processing speed, cooperation tendencies, response to authority, memory, objectives, relationships and prior experience.

The simulation changes the environment. A communication fails. A supply line is interrupted. An adversary changes strategy. A civilian population moves. A drone operator loses visibility. An autonomous system makes an unexpected decision.

The value of the simulation depends on whether the human actors behave plausibly.

Real humans → controlled game environments → behavioural telemetry → behavioural models → synthetic human agents → real-world simulations → new data → better behavioural models.

23 / Thesis

The thesis.

The evidence does not support the simplistic statement that gaming makes people better at the real world.

It supports something considerably more interesting.

Games create environments in which humans repeatedly make observable decisions under controlled conditions. Those environments can be used to train capabilities, identify talent, measure behaviour, modify behaviour and study the structure of human decision-making.

The first four applications already exist. The fifth is emerging. The sixth, using behavioural models to populate real-world simulations, remains largely open.

The gaming industry may have spent three decades building something it did not initially realise it had built: a global infrastructure for observing human behaviour.

Conclusion

The distinction between game and real-world system becomes less important.

The most important change is conceptual.

For years, the argument for gaming's real-world value has centred on transfer. A gamer learns something in a virtual world and carries it into reality. That is real, but incomplete.

Reality can be represented inside a controlled environment. Humans can then be placed inside that environment and observed as they make decisions. Their behaviour can be measured, compared, manipulated and modelled.

The game becomes a laboratory. The player becomes a source of behavioural data. The resulting model can eventually become part of a simulation.

What matters is whether the environment contains the right rules, incentives, uncertainty and consequences to elicit behaviour that tells us something about the world outside it.

Research notes / evidence base

Peer-reviewed experimental evidence: game-based surgical situational-awareness training; game-based trauma-triage training; UAS operator selection comparing gamers and pilots; MMO behaviour and offline leadership; sequential human decision modelling; novel-game decision-making research.

Large-scale behavioural datasets: Sea Hero Quest; Foldit; EVE Online Project Discovery.

Institutional deployments: FAA gamer recruitment campaign for air traffic control; US Army immersive training; DARPA Strategic Social Interaction Modules.

Natural experiment: World of Warcraft Corrupted Blood incident and subsequent behavioural/epidemiological research.
