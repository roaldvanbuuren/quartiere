IKEA, Klarna and the people decision

Jan Hoffmann · 27 September 2026

Two well-known European companies put AI on the same job at roughly the same time.

The job was the least loved one in any consumer business: answering routine customer questions. Where is my order? When does the store open? How do I return this? Both companies had huge volumes of it, both built an AI assistant to take it over, and both assistants worked.

What happened next is the most useful lesson I know about AI adoption, because the technology was the same in both. What differed was a decision about people.

Klarna counted the saving

In early 2024 Klarna announced that its AI assistant was doing the work of 700 customer service agents. The company had paused hiring and was open about the goal: a leaner business, with AI carrying the load.

By May 2025 the tone had changed. Klarna's chief executive, Sebastian Siemiatkowski, told reporters that "really investing in the quality of the human support is the way of the future for us", and that customers need to know there will always be a human if they want one. Klarna started recruiting people for customer service again, through a flexible remote model.

To be fair to Klarna, it did not switch the AI off. The assistant still handles around two thirds of customer conversations, and response times are far better than before. The part that went wrong was the assumption underneath: that every question the AI answered was a person the company no longer needed. Customers noticed the difference in quality, and the company had to walk it back in public.

IKEA asked what people could do next

IKEA introduced its assistant, Billie, in 2021. In its first two years Billie handled about 47% of customer questions. Today the figure is around 74%.

On paper, that is a large call centre with much less to do. IKEA's answer was to ask what those people could do instead. Over roughly two years it retrained about 8,500 call centre employees as remote interior design advisors, helping customers plan kitchens, bedrooms and whole homes over video.

That remote channel now runs from 24 centres serving 31 countries. It brought in about €1.25 billion last financial year, up from €1.08 billion the year before, and IKEA reports customer satisfaction rising from 60% to 89% since Billie arrived.

The assistant took the questions nobody enjoyed answering. The people moved into work customers value more, and that the people themselves could grow into.

Three things IKEA got right

It decided what people would do before the machine took their work. The redeployment was part of the plan from the start. That is what makes people willing to use the tool: they can see where it leaves them.

It treated freed-up time as capacity. Klarna's number was 700 agents saved. IKEA's number is a sales channel worth more than a billion euros a year. Saving the cost of a team is a one-off. Putting that team on higher-value work compounds.

It invested in the transition. Retraining 8,500 people over two years is expensive and slow, and it is the part most AI programmes skip. They buy the tool, run a training day, and wonder why nothing changes.

What this means for everyone else

Most companies are about to face their own version of this choice, usually one team at a time rather than 8,500 people at once. The customer support desk, the quoting team, the engineers answering the same technical questions every week.

The questions worth asking are the ones IKEA asked. Which parts of this job does the machine take? What could these people do with the time, that customers or colleagues would value more? What do they need to learn to get there, and who will help them through it?

Answer those first and the technology tends to get used, because people can see what is in it for them. Skip them and you risk the other outcome: a tool that works, a team that resists it, and a quality problem you only see once customers start complaining.

The same technology can give you either result. The deciding factor is what you choose to do with the people.
